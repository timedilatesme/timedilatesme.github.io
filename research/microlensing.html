Quasar microlensing

Stars in a lens galaxy make the images of a background quasar brighten and fade. How sharply they do so depends on the size of the quasar's accretion disk, which is far too small to resolve directly.

1 · One quasar, four images

A lensed quasar

A quasar is the bright, compact centre of a galaxy, powered by gas accreting onto a supermassive black hole. Behind a lens it can appear as four point-like images, with its host galaxy stretched into faint arcs.

2 · Zoom into one image

Stars break it up again

Each image passes through the stars of the lens galaxy. Every star acts as a small lens and splits the image into micro-images only microarcseconds apart. We can't resolve them, but we can measure how their total brightness changes.

3 · The source plane

A caustic network

Mapped onto the quasar's plane, the stars form a web of caustics, curves of very high magnification. As the accretion disk moves across it, the image brightens and fades.

The disk is larger at longer wavelengths (R ∝ λ4/3 for a thin disk). The small blue region shows sharp caustic crossings, and the larger red region smooths them out.

4 · Moving stars

The network keeps changing

The stars in the lens galaxy have their own motions, so the caustic network itself changes over time, on top of the disk's motion across it. Here the map is recomputed from 8 million rays every frame, and the view follows the disk.

In short

Flickering images

A quasar is the bright centre of a distant galaxy, powered by gas falling onto a supermassive black hole. When a galaxy lies in front of it, the quasar can appear as two or four images.

Each image passes through the stars of the lens galaxy, and each star acts as a small lens. Mapped onto the quasar's plane, the stars form a network of caustics: curves where the magnification is very high. As the quasar, the lens galaxy and its stars move relative to each other, the images brighten and fade independently.

A small source crosses a caustic sharply, a large one smoothly. The accretion disk is larger at longer wavelengths, so comparing how the images vary in different bands constrains the disk's size and its temperature profile. The quasar's own variability appears in every image, offset by the time delays; separating it from microlensing is part of the problem.

Because the stars move, the caustic network itself changes. With J. Fagin and collaborators I work on simulating this dynamical microlensing quickly on GPUs, and on showing that neglecting it can bias accretion-disk size measurements across a population of lensed quasars.
